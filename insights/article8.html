The New Law’s Bracket Changes: What Portfolios Should Do Before Year-End

The 2026 brackets are set, the SALT cap has quadrupled, and a new deduction exists for anyone over 65. The portfolios that adjust before December 31 keep the difference. The ones that wait pay it.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

In the first week of December 2026, an adviser in Naples sat down with a couple, both 68, retired, living on a pension, Social Security, and a portfolio of index funds and municipal bonds. The adviser had one question for them, and it was not about their asset allocation. It was about their tax bracket, because the bracket had moved, and the move changed what the portfolio should do before the year ended. The couple’s taxable income was $210,000, which put them in the 24 percent bracket for 2026, a bracket that tops out at $403,550 for a married couple. The adviser’s recommendation was not to sell anything. It was to convert $190,000 of their traditional IRA to a Roth, filling the 24 percent bracket to its top, because the conversion would be taxed at 24 percent now and never again, while the same dollars left in the traditional IRA would be taxed at whatever rate applied when the required minimum distributions began. The couple had never thought of their tax bracket as a portfolio decision. In 2026, it is the portfolio decision.

The law that moved the brackets is the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and the 2026 figures are now final. The standard deduction for 2026 is $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly, $16,100 for a single filer, and $24,150 for a head of household, under Revenue Procedure 2025-32. The top rate of 37 percent applies above $768,700 for a married couple and $640,600 for a single filer. The 22 percent bracket tops out at $211,400 for a married couple, and the 24 percent bracket at $403,550. These are not dramatic changes from 2025. They are the inflation-adjusted version of a tax code that is now, for the first time in a decade, stable, and the stability is the opportunity: a portfolio decision made in December 2026 will still be the right decision in December 2027, because the brackets are no longer scheduled to change.

The SALT cap’s quiet quadrupling

The largest single change in the new law is the one that has received the least attention from portfolio managers, and it is the state and local tax deduction. The cap, which was $10,000 from 2018 through 2025, is $40,400 for 2026, and it phases down for taxpayers with modified adjusted gross income above $505,000, with a floor of $10,000. For a married couple in a high-tax state with $40,000 of state income and property tax, the deduction is now worth four times what it was, and at a 24 percent marginal rate, the additional $30,400 of deduction is worth about $7,300 a year in federal tax.

The portfolio implication is not about the deduction itself. It is about the municipal bond. A couple that can now deduct $40,400 of state tax has less need for the tax exemption on municipal bond interest, because the federal deduction is doing part of the work the exemption used to do. The break-even yield between a municipal bond and a taxable bond moves, and for a couple in the 24 percent bracket, the taxable bond becomes competitive at a yield the municipal bond cannot match. The portfolios that hold municipal bonds because they always have, without re-running the break-even, are holding an allocation priced for a tax code that no longer exists.

The senior deduction and the 35 percent cap

The new law adds a deduction that did not exist before: $6,000 per person aged 65 or older, available through 2028, on top of the standard deduction. For the couple in the opening story, both 68, that is $12,000 of additional deduction, which at a 24 percent rate is worth about $2,900 a year. The deduction phases out at higher incomes, and it expires after 2028 unless Congress extends it, which means it is a planning window, not a permanent feature, and the portfolios that use it should use it while it exists.

The same law takes something away, and it takes it from the taxpayers who itemize the most. The benefit of itemized deductions is now capped at 35 percent for taxpayers in the 37 percent bracket, which means a dollar of charitable giving, mortgage interest, or state tax deducted by a taxpayer in the top bracket saves 35 cents instead of 37. The difference is two cents on the dollar, and it is small, but it changes the arithmetic of bunching: a taxpayer who bunches two years of charitable giving into one year, to exceed the standard deduction, now gets slightly less for the bunch, and the charitable deduction itself is subject to a new floor of 0.5 percent of adjusted gross income, which means the first half-percent of giving is not deductible at all.

The case for doing nothing

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because it is the argument most portfolios will follow by default, and it is not wrong. The bracket changes are small. The standard deduction covers most taxpayers, and most taxpayers do not itemize, which means the SALT cap and the 35 percent cap do not touch them. The senior deduction is worth a few thousand dollars, not a fortune. A portfolio that does nothing before year-end, that holds its municipal bonds, skips the Roth conversion, and lets the brackets take care of themselves, will not be materially worse off in January than it was in December. The tax code rewards the patient, and the patient do nothing.

The counterargument fails on the compounding, not the arithmetic. A Roth conversion of $190,000 at 24 percent, done once, is a single decision, but the dollars it moves will compound tax-free for twenty years, and the difference between tax-free compounding and taxable compounding over twenty years is measured in tens of thousands of dollars, not hundreds. A municipal bond allocation that is no longer optimal, held for a decade, costs a few tenths of a percent a year, which is a few thousand dollars a year on a million-dollar portfolio, and a few thousand dollars a year for a decade is a real number. The year-end decisions are small individually. They are large in aggregate, and the aggregate is what the portfolio keeps.

The year-end checklist

The practical version fits on a single page, and it starts with the bracket. Find the top of the taxpayer’s current bracket, and fill it: convert enough of the traditional IRA to a Roth to reach the top of the 22 or 24 percent bracket, but not into the 32 percent bracket, because the jump from 24 to 32 is the steepest in the code. Harvest gains at the 0 percent rate, where the last article in this series showed a retired couple could realize $76,400 of long-term gains tax-free, and buy the same funds back with a higher basis. Bunch charitable giving above the 0.5 percent floor, and consider a donor-advised fund, which lets the deduction be taken in the bunching year while the grants are made over time.

The last item on the checklist is the one the new law made urgent: the Roth catch-up rule. Starting in 2026, employees whose FICA wages exceeded $150,000 in 2025 must make their catch-up contributions as Roth contributions, not pre-tax. A 60-year-old executive earning $200,000 who has been making pre-tax catch-up contributions for years will find, in January, that the option has closed, and the payroll department will have made the change without asking. The portfolios that check the payroll election before year-end will not be surprised. The ones that do not will discover the change on the first pay stub of the year, when it is too late to plan around it.
