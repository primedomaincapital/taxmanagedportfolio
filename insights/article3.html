Tax-Managed Investing for High Earners: 2026 Guide

The 2026 tax code is set: 37% top rate, 20% capital gains, 3.8% NIIT, and new deduction caps. A high-earner's playbook for keeping more of every dollar.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

The most important tax event of 2026 already happened — in July 2025. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the 2017 rate structure permanent, ending years of sunset anxiety. The top ordinary rate stays at 37%. Long-term capital gains stay at 0%, 15%, and 20%. Without the bill, the top ordinary rate would have reverted to 39.6% in 2026 and the top capital-gains rate would have jumped to 25% — a $50,000 difference on a $1 million gain. Planning can finally use multi-year horizons without legislative guesswork. That stability is the foundation everything below is built on, and it is worth more than any single strategy: the most expensive tax mistakes are the ones made rushing to beat a deadline that no longer exists.

Here is the 2026 rate board, in one paragraph. Ordinary income tops out at 37% — for single filers above about $640,600 of taxable income, joint filers above about $768,700. Long-term gains and qualified dividends: 0% up to $49,450 single / $98,900 joint, 15% through $545,500 / $613,700, then 20%. The 3.8% net investment income tax still applies above $200,000 single / $250,000 joint of modified AGI, putting the all-in federal rate on investment income at 23.8% for top-bracket investors. Short-term gains are ordinary income — up to 37%, plus NIIT. The one-year holding line remains the cheapest tax-planning tool in existence: hold twelve months and a day, and the rate on the gain falls from 37% to 23.8%. Nothing else in this guide saves that much that fast.

Three Strategies That Earn Their Fees

First, asset location — the highest-return tax strategy nobody charges you for. Interest and short-term gains belong in tax-deferred accounts; qualified dividends and long-term appreciation belong in taxable accounts where the 20%/23.8% rates apply. Bonds in the 401(k), equities in the brokerage account — the textbook answer exists because the math is overwhelming. A high earner holding a total bond fund in taxable at a 37% marginal rate is donating nearly two-fifths of the yield to the Treasury. The location decision is worth more than most fund selection decisions, and it costs nothing to implement.

Second, systematic tax-loss harvesting through direct ownership. The pooled-fund problem: you cannot harvest inside an ETF or mutual fund. The solution is a separately managed account or direct-indexing portfolio holding individual securities, where single-stock losses can be realized continuously. The scale of professional harvesting is now industrial — one major manager realized $8.8 billion in losses in 2025 alone, worth an estimated $3.3 billion in tax benefit — and the economics favor high earners disproportionately, because each harvested dollar offsets gains taxed at up to 37%. Pair harvesting with charitable giving and the deferred gain can disappear permanently: donate the appreciated replacement shares, take the deduction, and the step-up in basis at death or the charity's tax-exempt sale erases what harvesting deferred. This is the closest thing to a free lunch in the tax code, and it is entirely legal.

Third, manage the new deduction limits. Starting in 2026, itemized deductions for top-bracket taxpayers are capped at a 35% benefit — you may be in the 37% bracket, but your deductions are worth 35 cents on the dollar. The SALT cap rises to $40,000 (phasing out above $500,000 of AGI, reverting to $10,000 in 2030). Charitable itemizers face a new 0.5%-of-AGI floor — on $2 million of AGI, the first $10,000 of giving generates no deduction — while non-itemizers get a new above-the-line deduction up to $1,000 single / $2,000 joint. The planning implications are concrete: bunch charitable gifts into alternating years with a donor-advised fund, time SALT payments against the phase-out, and recognize that the marginal value of every deduction dollar has fallen. Strategies built on the old 37-cents-on-the-dollar assumption need repricing.

The Geography of the Portfolio

Two more items belong in every high earner's 2026 review. State taxes: a California resident in the top bracket pays 13.3% on top of federal — an all-in marginal rate on short-term gains above 50%. New York City residents face a combined state-and-city load near 11%. Asset location and entity planning (trusts, in particular, hit the 37% bracket at far lower income thresholds than individuals — the compressed trust brackets remain one of the code's nastiest traps) matter enormously by zip code. And QSBS: the bill expanded qualified small business stock benefits for shares issued after July 4, 2025, creating a dual-track system — founders and early employees should verify which track their shares ride, because the exclusion can shelter up to $10 million of gain per issuer.

The through-line of 2026 planning is this: the code rewards patience, structure, and ownership — and punishes haste, pooling, and neglect, in that order. Hold for the long-term rate. Own securities directly so losses are harvestable. Locate assets where the code treats them best. Bunch what can be bunched. None of this requires exotic products. It requires a manager who treats the tax return as part of the investment process rather than an afterthought — because for a high earner, the after-tax return is the only return that spends.
