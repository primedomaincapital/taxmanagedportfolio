How Much Tax Alpha Does Direct Indexing Actually Deliver?

The pitch says 1–2% a year. Vanguard's own model says 0.47–1.27%. The gap between the two numbers is where the real answer lives.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

In a glass conference room above Park Avenue this autumn, an adviser clicked to the slide every direct-indexing pitch ends on. One number, set in 48-point type: 1–2% a year in tax alpha. No asterisk visible from the back row. No horizon, no bracket, no fee netted out. The prospective client, a founder holding most of a business-sale windfall in a taxable account, asked the only question that mattered: whose number is that, and what did they actually measure? The room went quiet for a beat too long.

The number on the slide is not invented. It is borrowed — from real research, by serious firms — and then stripped of every condition that made it true. The published estimates span roughly half a percent a year at the sober end to two percent at the promotional end, and both ends cite real work. The honest answer is not a number. It is a set of conditions, and the investor who does not know them is buying the slide, not the strategy.

Start with the most careful estimate in the field. Vanguard's July 2024 research, "Tax-Loss Harvesting: Why a Personalized Approach Is Important," by Thomas Paradise, Kevin Khang, and Joel Dickson, modeled roughly 10,000 simulations over market history from January 1982 through March 2023, using Axioma data. Its headline finding: tax-loss harvesting alpha — the additional annual after-tax return from a well-run program — ranges from 0.47% to 1.27% over a 15-year horizon. The low end describes a mass-affluent investor at a 22% ordinary rate and a 15% long-term rate; the high end, higher brackets and larger taxable portfolios. Three categories of inputs — investor characteristics, investor behavior, and market environment — each drive roughly one-third of the variation in value. And the single most important behavior, by Vanguard's analysis, is the reinvestment of tax savings back into the portfolio. Harvest a loss, bank the refund, spend it, and much of the benefit evaporates.

Then read the fine print Vanguard itself insists on. Those percentages apply only to the taxable equity assets being harvested — not to the whole portfolio. Vanguard's own illustration makes the point bluntly: if an investor's tax-loss harvesting alpha is 1%, but taxable equities are only 25% of the portfolio, the expected impact on the total portfolio is 0.25%. A 1% headline becomes a quarter of a percent the moment it meets a real balance sheet. The pitch deck rarely shows that line.

The rest of the published range tells the same story from different angles. Parametric Portfolio Associates, the direct-indexing pioneer now inside Morgan Stanley, has estimated up to 2% in annual tax alpha. Cerulli Associates told advisers to expect an extra 100 to 200 basis points of after-tax return — research director Tom O'Shea's framing in the firm's 2022 white paper. Wealthfront, analyzing more than 100,000 of its own client accounts over ten years, reported 1.55% annualized in the top tax bracket; Betterment, working at the ETF level, reported 0.77% over five years. And research summarized from Morningstar and Parametric puts systematic harvesting at 0.80% to 1.08% a year during the first three to five years of a portfolio's life. None of these numbers is wrong. They measure different investors, over different periods, at different levels of execution — and the 2% figure, in particular, describes the most favorable combination of all three.

The market year decides the harvest

The variable the pitch decks mention least is the one that moves the number most: what the market did. Tax-loss harvesting converts dispersion into tax assets, and dispersion is not evenly distributed across calendar years. The S&P 500's total returns tell the recent story in four numbers, from Aswath Damodaran's historical dataset at NYU Stern: 2022, down 18.04%; 2023, up 26.06%; 2024, up 24.88%; 2025, up 17.78%. A portfolio funded in January 2022 was rich in losses within months. A portfolio funded in January 2023 watched the index rise roughly 90% cumulatively over the next three years, and its harvesting opportunities came only from individual stocks that fell while the market rose, plus fresh contributions buying new lots that later dipped.

The bull-market years still yielded something. A 2023 study in The Journal of Beta Investment Strategies found that a direct-indexed portfolio harvests 1.9 to 2.1 times more capital loss than an ETF-based strategy over ten years — because single stocks fall even when the index rises. In 2024, when the index gained nearly 25%, dozens of its constituents still finished down, and the direct indexer harvested those. The ETF holder cannot; the fund's internal losses are invisible to the shareholder. But the scale differs enormously: harvesting in 2022 meant selling positions down 20% and 30%, while harvesting in 2024 meant picking off single-stock declines of 8% and 12% while the portfolio's basis climbed. Same machinery, different weather, different yield.

Vanguard's scenario work puts the benefit at 0.51% to 1.4% in high-volatility 15-year environments and 0.08% to 0.83% in average-volatility ones. The famous 100 basis points lives at the top of the volatile case. An investor who funds an account at the start of a calm decade should expect the bottom of the range, not the middle.

Three discounts on the headline number

The 1–2% headline takes three discounts before it becomes a number an investor can spend. The first is scaling: alpha applies to the taxable equity sleeve, not the whole portfolio, so a 1% harvesting alpha is 25 basis points at the portfolio level for the mass-affluent investor Vanguard modeled. The second discount is time. Harvesting is richest in a portfolio's early years, when cost bases are fresh, and it fades as the portfolio matures: bases rise with the market, embedded gains accumulate, and the remaining losses thin out. Fresh contributions renew the opportunity, which is why the strategy favors investors still adding money over investors drawing down. The third discount is the fee. Morningstar's 2023 survey found direct-indexing fees starting at 0.25% for some offerings with $250,000 minimums and running to 0.40% for providers serving smaller accounts; Charles Schwab has described typical fees of 0.30% to 0.40%, against about 0.03% for a plain S&P 500 ETF. Take Vanguard's low-end 0.47% gross alpha, subtract a 0.35% fee, and 12 basis points remain — before tracking error, before state tax, before the adviser's own fee on top.

None of this makes the ETF alternative tax-free. Dimensional Fund Advisors, in research published in November 2026, found that even index funds with expense ratios of just 3 basis points carried annualized tax costs of approximately 38 to 40 basis points over the five years ending in 2025 — the tax drag of a pooled fund is real, and it is larger than its fee. Dimensional's work also suggests that flexibility in how an index is tracked can cut tax costs by as much as 5 basis points a year for a U.S. portfolio and 20 basis points for an international one. The honest comparison is never 1% against zero. It is the net harvesting benefit, after fees and tracking error, against the 38 to 40 basis points the cheap ETF already imposes. For the right investor, direct indexing still wins that comparison by a wide margin. For the wrong one, it loses it.

The counterargument: the case that tax alpha is overstated

The skeptics have a real argument, and it deserves more than a footnote. A separate analysis in The Journal of Beta Investment Strategies concluded that management fees can make tax-loss harvesting inferior to a plain index ETF, even under favorable assumptions — the fee is certain, the harvest is not. Morningstar's 2023 simulations add a second warning: in a 20-year simulation, a portfolio carrying 2% tracking error ended at between $590,000 and $770,000 on a $100,000 starting investment, against $670,000 for the benchmark. The tax benefit, in other words, can be swamped by the portfolio drifting from its index — and aggressive harvesting, with its constant substitution of near-identical securities, is exactly what creates drift. Good managers control it with optimization constraints. Bad ones do not.

There is also the deferral truth the pitch decks skip. Harvesting does not eliminate tax; it postpones it. Sell at a loss, buy a replacement at a lower basis, and the future gain is larger by exactly the amount harvested. The benefit is the time value of that deferral, plus the chance to use losses against higher-rate gains, donate appreciated replacement shares, or pass them to heirs with a stepped-up basis. Vanguard's suboptimal-program finding makes the same point from the other direction: quarterly harvesting, reinvesting only half the tax savings, inside a commingled fund, produced 0.04% to 0.13% a year — essentially nothing. Execution is not a detail. It is most of the return.

What to ask before you pay for it

None of this is an argument against direct indexing. It is an argument against buying it on a single number. The investor who benefits most is easy to describe, and Vanguard's, Cerulli's, and Morningstar's work all describe the same person: a high-bracket investor with a large share of wealth in taxable equities, regular capital gains elsewhere to shelter — a concentrated position, a business sale, real estate, private equity distributions — a long horizon, and fresh contributions still flowing in. Cerulli's growth figures show the industry knows it: direct indexing stood at about $462 billion at the end of 2022, was projected to reach roughly $825 billion by the end of 2026 at a 12.3% annual growth rate, and, according to MMI-Cerulli data, reached $1.2 trillion by the first quarter of 2026 as the largest manager-traded SMA strategy.

Before signing, ask for four numbers in writing. The expected harvesting benefit net of the manager's fee, for your bracket, not the top bracket. The expected tracking error, net of fees and taxes, over full market cycles — not the gross harvested losses. The benefit scaled to your taxable share of total wealth, not to the harvested sleeve alone. And the benefit for a portfolio funded the year you fund it, in a calm market as well as a volatile one. If a manager cannot produce those four numbers, they are selling the slide. If they can, the 1–2% claim may turn out to be true — for you, in your years, at your bracket.
