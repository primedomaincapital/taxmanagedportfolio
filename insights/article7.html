Tax-Loss Harvesting vs. Tax-Gain Harvesting: When Selling Winners Pays

Loss harvesting defers tax. Gain harvesting erases it. In the low-income years, selling winners at the 0% rate beats deferring them.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

In July 2026, USA Today walked through the tax return of a retired couple, both 67, living on $70,000 a year from a pension and IRA withdrawals. Their taxable income before any stock sales was $22,500, and the top of the 0% capital-gains bracket for a married couple in 2026 is $98,900. The arithmetic that followed was the kind that makes a reader put the paper down and call an adviser: the couple could sell a fund, realize a $76,400 long-term gain, and owe zero federal tax on it. Not deferred. Not reduced. Zero — and then buy the same fund back the same afternoon with a cost basis $76,400 higher than before.

Tax-loss harvesting is the famous half of the trade: sell losers, bank the loss, shelter gains. Tax-gain harvesting is the other half: sell winners on purpose, in a year your income is low enough that the gain is taxed at 0%, and buy them straight back. The wash-sale rule — the 30-day trap that governs loss harvesting — does not apply to gains at all. There is no waiting period, no substitute security, no tracking error. You can sell an index fund at 10 a.m. and repurchase it at 10:05, and the IRS has no complaint. The benefit is permanent: a $30,000 gain realized at 0% with the basis reset $30,000 higher is $30,000 that never returns as taxable income.

The window is narrow: the year after the paycheck stops and before the required withdrawals start, the sabbatical year, the layoff year, the startup year when the business pays nothing, a child's custodial account. And it closes on December 31 — the gain must be realized inside the low-income calendar year, and unused room does not carry forward.

The 0% bracket, in plain numbers

For 2026, the 0% rate on long-term capital gains applies to taxable income up to $49,450 single, $66,200 head of household, and $98,900 joint, under Revenue Procedure 2025-32 and IRS Topic 409. The 15% rate then runs to $545,500 single and $613,700 joint. The threshold is measured on taxable income — income after deductions — which is why the room is larger than it looks. The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for joint filers, so a filer with no other income can realize $65,550 of long-term gains as a single filer, or $131,100 as a couple, and pay no federal capital-gains tax at all. Ordinary income fills the bracket first: wages, pensions, IRA withdrawals, and interest claim the room from the bottom, and gains stack on top. The 3.8% net investment income tax applies only above $200,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers, so it is not a factor at these income levels.

Two details decide whether the maneuver works. First, the gain must be long-term — the asset held more than a year. A short-term gain is ordinary income, taxed at rates up to 37%, and no bracket maneuver rescues it. Second, the repurchase is immediate and identical. The portfolio does not change at all — only the basis moves.

The four windows where selling winners pays

The most reliable window is the retirement gap. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, required minimum distributions begin at age 73 for people born from 1951 through 1959, and at 75 for anyone born in 1960 or later. Retire at 62 or 65, and the years before the first RMD are often the lowest-income years of an investor's adult life. The first RMD is modest, about 3.8% of the prior year-end balance at 73 under the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table, but the percentage climbs every birthday, and missing one carries a 25% excise tax, 10% if corrected within two years. The gap years are the time to use the 0% room — once RMDs, Social Security, and pension income stack up, it is gone for good. "Compelling, especially in lower income years, like if you lose a job or in early retirement bridge years, before Social Security kicks in and before RMDs," is how Emily Shacklett, a certified public accountant and wealth adviser at Hightower, put it to USA Today in July 2026. Kevin Knull, chief executive of TaxStatus, made the same point in plainer terms: in a year when taxable income falls below the threshold, "investors can deliberately sell appreciated stock or fund shares, pay nothing in federal tax on the gain, and buy the same investment right back."

The second window is the interrupted career: a layoff, a sabbatical, a founder's year between ventures, a phased retirement to part-time. Nobody schedules a layoff, but the tax return does not care about the cause. A single filer earning $30,000 in a part-time year has $13,900 of taxable income after the $16,100 standard deduction, leaving $35,550 of 0% room for gains. The third window belongs to children. Wealthfront launched custodial accounts in June 2026 built around automatic tax-gain harvesting: for 2026, the first $1,350 of a child's unearned income is tax-free, the next $1,350 is taxed at the child's own rate — which for long-term gains can be 0% — and anything above $2,700 is taxed at the parents' marginal rate under the kiddie-tax rules. The amounts are small, but they recur every year. Gift-tax limits still apply: $19,000 per recipient from an individual, $38,000 from a married couple, in 2026.

The fourth window is estate planning, and it cuts in the opposite direction. Under Section 1014, assets held at death pass to heirs with their basis reset to fair market value, erasing a lifetime of unrealized gain entirely. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the federal estate exemption permanent at $15 million per person, $30 million for a married couple, from 2026, with a 40% rate only above it — up from $13.99 million in 2025, replacing a scheduled fall to roughly $7 million. For an estate comfortably below $15 million, the step-up already erases the gain for free at death, and harvesting gains during life spends 0% room to solve a problem the step-up would have solved anyway. Gain harvesting earns its keep when the owner might sell during life or when the estate exceeds the exemption. The step-up is the reason to hold. Gain harvesting is the reason to sell — and knowing which applies is the whole decision.

Loss harvesting vs. gain harvesting: the real comparison

Put the two strategies side by side and the asymmetry is striking. Loss harvesting defers tax: you sell low, buy a replacement at a lower basis, and the tax comes due later, larger. Its value is the time value of the deferral, and it is capped in a way gain harvesting is not — net capital losses offset only $3,000 of ordinary income a year, with the rest carried forward, and every harvested loss must survive the 30-day wash-sale rule across all your accounts. Gain harvesting extinguishes tax: the gain realized at 0% never returns, with no wash-sale rule and no annual cap beyond the bracket room. What it has, instead, is a rival claimant for the same room. Every dollar of 0% bracket space used to harvest a gain is a dollar that cannot be used for a Roth conversion taxed at 10% or 12% — and for many retirees in the gap years, the conversion is the better use, because it moves money out of the future RMD stream permanently rather than merely resetting a basis. The right question is never "should I harvest gains?" It is "gains or conversions — which use of this year's room buys more?"

State tax is the half of the comparison the federal arithmetic hides. Most states tax capital gains as ordinary income, with no 0% carve-out — a gain that is free federally is taxed in California at up to 13.3% and in New York at the state rate plus, in the city, more. A couple harvesting $76,400 at 0% federally can still owe several thousand dollars to Sacramento or Albany.

The counterargument: when selling winners backfires

There are good reasons not to harvest, and the first is the step-up: for assets you will hold until death inside the exemption, harvesting is motion without progress. The second is the AGI side effect. Realized gains raise adjusted gross income even when the federal tax on them is zero, and AGI governs the taxation of Social Security benefits, Medicare's IRMAA premium surcharges, and Affordable Care Act premium subsidies, which phase out as income rises. A harvest that saves future capital-gains tax and costs thousands a year in lost subsidies and higher Part B premiums is a loss dressed as a win. The third reason is the conversion trade-off: in the gap years, the 0% and 12% brackets are a wasting asset, and spending them on gains the step-up would have erased anyway, while pre-tax IRA balances compound into larger future RMDs, is the most common way this strategy goes wrong.

There is also a simpler objection: the maneuver bets on the future being worse than the present. Harvesting a gain at 15% today only pays if the rate at eventual sale would have been higher. Rates can fall. A move to Florida or Texas can erase the state bill. Gain harvesting at 0% needs no such bet: zero is zero under any future law. Gain harvesting at 15% is a forecast, and forecasts about tax law have a poor record.

What to do before December 31

The window is a calendar-year window, and the deadline is the hard kind. First, measure the room: the 0% threshold for your filing status, plus your standard deduction, minus your other income for the year. Second, check the state tax and the AGI side effects on subsidies, premiums, and Social Security taxation. Third, compare the gain harvest against a Roth conversion for the same room. Fourth, sell and rebuy the same security the same day — there is no wash-sale rule for gains, and waiting only adds market risk. Fifth, keep the records: the new basis is the whole point. This is general information, not tax advice — the interaction with Social Security, Medicare, and state law turns on individual circumstances.

Three strong market years (the S&P 500 gained 26.06% in 2023, 24.88% in 2024, 17.78% in 2025) have left most taxable portfolios sitting on embedded gains larger than at any point in the last decade. The investors who will pay the least tax on those gains are not the ones who avoid selling. They are the ones who sell in the years when selling is free.
